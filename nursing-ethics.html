Unit I (1 MCQ)

Introduction to Nursing Ethics

1. Core Terminology Definitions

  • Ethics: System of moral principles governing behavior and decision-making regarding what is right and wrong.
  • Nursing Ethics: Specialized branch of bioethics focusing on moral duties, values, and decision-making within nursing practice.
  • Bioethics: Ethical analysis of moral issues arising in biology, medicine, and healthcare technology.
  • Value: Personal or professional belief about the worth of a given idea, attitude, custom, or object.
  • Belief: An acceptance that a statement is true or that something exists.
  • Morals: Private, personal standards of right and wrong built on social, cultural, or religious norms.
  • Ethical Dilemma: Situation where two or more moral principles conflict, with no clean, risk-free outcome.

2. Importance of Ethics in Nursing

Guides clinical decision-making, protects patient rights, maintains public trust, and establishes professional accountability.

Unit II (1 MCQ | 0.5 SEQ)

Value Set & Transmission

1. Values & Value Clarification

Value Clarification: Process by which people identify, assess, and develop their own personal and professional values to prevent personal bias from interfering with non-judgmental care.

Types of Values

Personal, Societal, Organizational, Professional, and Moral Values.

Modes of Value Transmission

Modeling, Moralizing, Laissez-Faire, Rewarding/Punishing, and Responsible Choice.

2. Implications in Diverse Societies

Nurses must deliver culturally congruent care while respecting diverse moral and religious worldviews without imposing personal values.

Unit III (2 MCQs | 0.5 SEQ) • Core Focus

Ethical Principles and Theories

Based on reference text: Principles of Biomedical Ethics (8th ed.) by Beauchamp T. L. and Childress J. F. (2019).

Figure 3.1: The Four Principles Framework (Beauchamp & Childress, 2019)

1. Principles in Healthcare & Ethical Theories

Autonomy

Respecting the self-determination of competent patients (e.g., Informed Consent, Refusal of Care).

Non-Maleficence

"Primum non-nocere" — Do no harm. Avoiding unnecessary risks, harm, or deliberate injury.

Beneficence

Promoting well-being by balancing clinical benefits against risks and burdens.

Justice

Fair distribution of healthcare resources, equality of treatment, and non-discrimination.

Theory Connections

  • Deontology (Kantian): Focuses on moral duty and rules regardless of consequences. Directly underpins Autonomy and Justice.
  • Utilitarianism (Consequentialism): Focuses on maximizing the greatest good for the greatest number. Underpins Beneficence and Non-Maleficence.
  • Virtue Ethics: Emphasizes the internal moral character and compassion of the healthcare provider.
📌 Key Exam Point: Beauchamp & Childress emphasize that these four principles are Prima Facie obligations—binding unless they directly conflict with an equal or weightier moral principle in a specific case.

2. Common Ethical Dilemmas

  • Autonomy vs. Paternalism: Competent client refuses life-saving care (e.g., refusal of blood transfusion).
  • Veracity (Truth-Telling) vs. Non-Maleficence: Family requests withholding a poor prognosis from a vulnerable patient.
  • Moral Distress: Nurse knows the ethically correct action but is unable to execute it due to institutional policy or orders.

3. Strategies to Resolve Dilemmas

  1. Consultation with Institutional Ethics Committees (IEC).
  2. Conducting multidisciplinary Ethics Rounds and debriefing sessions.
  3. Utilizing standard Professional Codes of Ethics (ICN / PNC).
  4. Facilitating open interprofessional communication and family conferences.

Unit IV (2 MCQs | 0.5 SEQ)

Confidentiality and Informed Consent

1. Definitions & Importance

Confidentiality: Duty to protect private patient health information from unauthorized disclosure.

Informed Consent: Voluntary authorization by a competent individual to undergo a medical procedure after understanding risks, benefits, and alternatives.

2. Essential Elements of Informed Consent

  • Disclosure: Patient receives clear explanation of procedure, risks, benefits, and options.
  • Comprehension: Patient understands the information provided.
  • Voluntariness: Decision made freely without coercion.
  • Competence: Patient has legal and mental capacity to make decisions.

3. Nurse’s Role in Informed Consent

The nurse acts as a witness to the signature, verifies patient comprehension, and advocates for patient questions before procedures.

Unit V (2 MCQs | 0.5 SEQ)

Bills of Rights

1. Patient's Rights in Tertiary Care

  • Right to respectful and compassionate care.
  • Right to full information regarding diagnosis and treatment plans.
  • Right to privacy and confidentiality of medical records.
  • Right to refuse medical treatment.
  • Right to access medical records and receive clear billing explanations.

2. Role of the Nurse

Nurses serve as primary client advocates ensuring patients understand and exercise their rights within healthcare institutions.

Unit VI (2 MCQs | 0.5 SEQ)

Code of Ethics

1. Elements & Functions of Codes

Provides professional standards, guides clinical conduct, informs the public of standards, and maintains accountability.

2. ICN vs. PNC Code Comparison

ICN Code of Ethics

Global standard focusing on 4 fundamental elements: Nurses & People, Practice, Profession, and Co-workers.

Pakistan Nursing Council (PNC) Code

National regulatory code emphasizing cultural sensitivity, professional scope, and legal responsibilities within Pakistan.

Unit VII (2 MCQs | 0.5 SEQ)

Professional Autonomy and Ethics

Key Concepts

  • Professional Autonomy: Ability to make independent clinical decisions within professional scope.
  • Accountability: Answerability for one's clinical decisions, actions, and outcomes.
  • Unity: Professional solidarity among nurses strengthening advocacy and practice standards.
Unit VIII (3 MCQs | High-Weight Unit)

Ethical Dilemma in Professional Practice

1. Areas of Negligence & Liability

Failure to follow standard operating procedures, medication administration errors, inadequate patient monitoring, and failure to document accurately.

2. Advocacy Scenarios

  • Life Support Equipment: Navigating end-of-life choices and DNR orders.
  • Risk Management: Identifying hazards and adhering to infection control protocols.
  • Documentation: Maintaining accurate medical records ("If it wasn't documented, it wasn't done").
  • Medical Malpractice: Understanding legal duty of care, breach of duty, causation, and damages.
