Unit I: Introduction to Nursing Ethics

1. Core Ethical Terminology (Definitions)

Ethics

The formal systematic study of moral beliefs, principles, and conduct; distinguishing right from wrong behavior in human actions.

Nursing Ethics

A specialized branch of bioethics that governs moral decisions, professional duties, and values specifically within the nurse-patient-system relationship.

Bio-Ethics

The broader study of ethical issues arising from advances in biology, medicine, and life sciences (e.g., organ transplantation, gene editing, end-of-life care).

Values

Enduring personal or professional beliefs about the worth, desirability, or importance of an idea, object, or behavior that guide decision-making.

Beliefs

Intellectual or emotional acceptance of something as true or real, with or without empirical evidence.

Morals

Personal or societal standards of right and wrong behavior deeply rooted in culture, religion, and family tradition.

Attitude

A mental position, feelings, or disposition toward a person, object, or situation that influences one's response.

Conflict

A struggle or clash resulting from incompatible or opposing needs, drives, values, or external demands.

Dilemma

A difficult situation in which a choice must be made between two options, both of which have undesirable consequences.

Ethical Dilemma

A complex situation where two or more moral principles conflict, and choosing one option forces the nurse to compromise another valid ethical duty.

2. Importance of Ethics in Nursing

  • Protects Vulnerable Patients: Safeguards patient autonomy and dignity when clients are incapacitated or at their most vulnerable.
  • Guides Clinical Decision-Making: Provides a structured standard for resolving complex healthcare situations without depending purely on emotional reactions.
  • Fosters Interprofessional Trust: Builds a culture of integrity, accountability, and respect between nurses, physicians, clients, and society.
  • Upholds Professional Boundaries: Defines legal and ethical parameters that prevent abuse of power, exploitation, and medical negligence.

3. Flexner's Criteria of a Profession Applied to Nursing

According to Flexner & Bixler, a true profession must satisfy distinct criteria:

  1. Well-Defined Body of Specialized Knowledge: Rooted in scientific theories, evidence-based nursing research, and clinical sciences.
  2. Specialized Higher Education: Academic training delivered in universities/institutions (e.g., BSN, MSN, PhD) rather than informal apprenticeships.
  3. Service Orientation & Altruism: Focuses on service to humanity and public welfare rather than financial gain alone.
  4. Autonomy and Self-Regulation: Maintains independent practice standards, licensing procedures, and professional regulatory boards (e.g., PNC).
  5. Code of Ethics: Guided by explicit moral codes (e.g., PNC & ICN Codes) that govern practitioner behaviors.
  6. Professional Organization: Represented by strong professional bodies that set policy and advance practice.

4. Characteristics of a Professional Nurse

Clinical Competence

Demonstrates mastery of clinical skills, critical thinking, and evidence-based practice to ensure client safety.

Compassion & Empathy

Delivers holistic, empathetic, and respectful care without discrimination or bias.

Accountability

Takes direct personal and legal responsibility for clinical actions, omissions, and decisions.

Integrity & Veracity

Upholds absolute honesty, maintains patient confidentiality, and remains transparent with healthcare teams.
