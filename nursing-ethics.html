1. Ethical Principles in Healthcare & Ethical Theories

In biomedical ethics, ethical theories offer broad philosophical foundations, while ethical principles function as actionable, practical tools applied in daily clinical nursing care.

Figure 3.1: The Four Core Biomedical Principles (Beauchamp & Childress, 2019)

The Four Core Principles

1. Autonomy

Respecting a competent patient's right to self-determination and independent choices (e.g., Informed Consent, Right to Refuse Treatment).

2. Non-Maleficence

"Primum non-nocere" — Above all, do no harm. Avoiding deliberate injury or unnecessary risks during medical care.

3. Beneficence

Promoting client well-being. Actions done to benefit others while balancing advantages against potential risks.

4. Justice

Fairness and equality in allocating healthcare resources, non-discrimination, and treating similar cases equally.

Connecting Principles to Ethical Theories

  • Deontology (Kantian Ethics): Asserts actions are duty-bound and inherently right or wrong regardless of consequences. Forms the foundation of Autonomy and Justice.
  • Utilitarianism (Consequentialism): Focuses on the "greatest good for the greatest number." Underpins Beneficence and Non-Maleficence by maximizing net outcomes.
  • Virtue Ethics: Focuses on internal moral traits (compassion, honesty, fidelity, advocacy) within the nurse.
📌 Key Exam Point: Beauchamp & Childress classify these 4 principles as Prima Facie duties—meaning they are morally binding unless they conflict with an equal or weightier obligation in a specific clinical case.

2. Ethical Dilemmas Faced by Nurses & Clients

An ethical dilemma arises when a nurse is caught between two morally justifiable choices, where opting for one forces the violation of another equal moral duty.

Common Clinical Examples

  • Autonomy vs. Paternalism: A fully competent adult client refuses emergency surgery or blood transfusion due to religious beliefs, but the healthcare team wants to perform it to save life.
  • Truth-Telling (Veracity) vs. Non-Maleficence: Family members ask nurses to withhold a terminal diagnosis from an elderly client to avoid severe distress.
  • Justice vs. Resource Scarcity: Deciding how to allocate critical care beds or ventilators during hospital emergencies.
  • Moral Distress in Nursing: Occurs when nurses recognize the ethically right action but are restricted from executing it due to hospital policy or institutional barriers.

3. Strategies to Resolve Ethical Dilemmas

Nurses use structured professional strategies to handle ethical disputes in clinical practice:

  1. Institutional Ethics Committees (IEC): Multidisciplinary boards (ethicists, nurses, doctors, legal experts) providing guidance and consensus on challenging ethical cases.
  2. Ethics Rounds & Debriefings: Open team discussions to evaluate moral challenges, share perspectives, and ease nurse moral distress.
  3. Professional Codes of Ethics: Relying on the International Council of Nurses (ICN) Code or national codes to establish boundaries and rights.
  4. Value Clarification: Helping clients and families identify their personal values and preferences regarding end-of-life or medical care.
  5. Interprofessional Communication: Arranging family-physician-nurse conferences early to set clear goals of care.

4. Steps of the Ethical Decision-Making Model

A systematic framework based on Beauchamp & Childress that aligns with the Nursing Process (ADPIE):

Figure 3.3: Systematic Ethical Decision-Making Model

Step 1: Data Assessment

Collect clinical details, patient capacity, family values, legal implications, and social factors.

Step 2: Identify Ethical Conflict

Pinpoint which core principles are in opposition (e.g., Autonomy vs. Beneficence).

Step 3: Explore Courses of Action

Brainstorm all possible interventions and evaluate benefits, risks, and burdens for each.

Step 4: Select & Implement

Execute the most ethically defensible choice with multidisciplinary support and client advocacy.

Step 5: Evaluate Outcomes

Review the consequences of the decision, assess client/family adaptation, and document lessons learned.
