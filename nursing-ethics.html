Unit I: Introduction to Nursing Ethics

     

1. Core Ethical Terminology (Definitions)

     
       
         

Ethics

         

The formal systematic study of moral beliefs, principles, and conduct; distinguishing right from wrong behavior in human actions.

       
       
         

Nursing Ethics

         

A specialized branch of bioethics that governs moral decisions, professional duties, and values specifically within the nurse-patient-system relationship.

       
       
         

Bio-Ethics

         

The broader study of ethical issues arising from advances in biology, medicine, and life sciences (e.g., organ transplantation, gene editing, end-of-life care).

       
       
         

Values

         

Enduring personal or professional beliefs about the worth, desirability, or importance of an idea, object, or behavior that guide decision-making.

       
       
         

Beliefs

         

Intellectual or emotional acceptance of something as true or real, with or without empirical evidence.

       
       
         

Morals

         

Personal or societal standards of right and wrong behavior deeply rooted in culture, religion, and family tradition.

       
       
         

Attitude

         

A mental position, feelings, or disposition toward a person, object, or situation that influences one's response.

       
       
         

Conflict

         

A struggle or clash resulting from incompatible or opposing needs, drives, values, or external demands.

       
       
         

Dilemma

         

A difficult situation in which a choice must be made between two options, both of which have undesirable consequences.

       
       
         

Ethical Dilemma

         

A complex situation where two or more moral principles conflict, and choosing one option forces the nurse to compromise another valid ethical duty.

       
     
     

2. Importance of Ethics in Nursing

     
           
  • Protects Vulnerable Patients: Safeguards patient autonomy and dignity when clients are incapacitated or at their most vulnerable.
    •        
  • Guides Clinical Decision-Making: Provides a structured standard for resolving complex healthcare situations without depending purely on emotional reactions.
    •        
  • Fosters Interprofessional Trust: Builds a culture of integrity, accountability, and respect between nurses, physicians, clients, and society.
    •        
  • Upholds Professional Boundaries: Defines legal and ethical parameters that prevent abuse of power, exploitation, and medical negligence.
    •

3. Flexner's Criteria of a Profession Applied to Nursing

     

According to Flexner & Bixler, a true profession must satisfy distinct criteria:

     
           
  1. Well-Defined Body of Specialized Knowledge: Rooted in scientific theories, evidence-based nursing research, and clinical sciences.
    2.        
  2. Specialized Higher Education: Academic training delivered in universities/institutions (e.g., BSN, MSN, PhD) rather than informal apprenticeships.
    3.        
  3. Service Orientation & Altruism: Focuses on service to humanity and public welfare rather than financial gain alone.
    4.        
  4. Autonomy and Self-Regulation: Maintains independent practice standards, licensing procedures, and professional regulatory boards (e.g., PNC).
    5.        
  5. Code of Ethics: Guided by explicit moral codes (e.g., PNC & ICN Codes) that govern practitioner behaviors.
    6.        
  6. Professional Organization: Represented by strong professional bodies that set policy and advance practice.
    7.      
     

4. Characteristics of a Professional Nurse

     
       
         

Clinical Competence

         

Demonstrates mastery of clinical skills, critical thinking, and evidence-based practice to ensure client safety.

       
       
         

Compassion & Empathy

         

Delivers holistic, empathetic, and respectful care without discrimination or bias.

       
       
         

Accountability

         

Takes direct personal and legal responsibility for clinical actions, omissions, and decisions.

       
       
         

Integrity & Veracity

         

Upholds absolute honesty, maintains patient confidentiality, and remains transparent with healthcare teams.

Unit II: Value Set & Value Clarification

     

1. Definition of Values & Value Clarification

     

Value: An enduring belief about the worth or importance of a particular idea, standard, or behavior that directs decision-making.

     

Value Clarification: A reflective process by which individuals examine, identify, and understand their own personal values, how those values were formed, and how they influence daily behavior and professional practice.

3. Modes of Value Transmission

     
       
         

1. Modeling

         

Children/students learn values by observing role models (parents, senior nursing mentors).

       
       
         

2. Moralizing

         

Teaching a strict value set directly, leaving no room for the individual to question or evaluate options.

       
       
         

3. Laissez-Faire

         

Unguided exploration of values where individuals pick values with zero guidance, leading to potential value confusion.

       
       
         

4. Reward and Punishment

         

Values are reinforced through rewards for acceptable behaviors and punishment/rebuke for unacceptable ones.

       
       
         

5. Responsible Choice (Preferred Method)

         

Encourages individuals to explore multiple choices, evaluate potential consequences, and independently choose values with adult guidance.

4. Value Conflicts and Implications to Nursing Practice

     

A value conflict occurs when a nurse's personal values clash with a patient's choices, institutional policies, or physician directives (e.g., personal religious objection to blood transfusions or terminal sedation).

     
        ⚠️ Clinical Implication: Unresolved value conflicts lead to moral distress, personal burnout, clinical errors, and compromised patient care. Nurses must separate personal values from professional duties to deliver non-judgmental client care.      
     

5. Essential AACN Professional Nursing Values

     
           
  • Altruism: Unselfish concern for the welfare and well-being of clients and communities.
    •        
  • Autonomy: Respecting patient rights to self-determination and independent healthcare decisions.
    •        
  • Human Dignity: Respecting the inherent worth and uniqueness of every individual client.
    •        
  • Integrity: Acting in accordance with an appropriate code of ethics and accepted standards of practice.
    •        
  • Social Justice: Upholding moral, legal, and humanistic principles of equal treatment and fairness in healthcare access.
    •      
     

6. Implications of Care Ethics in a Diverse Society

     

In multicultural healthcare environments, nurses must practice cultural humility and transcultural care ethics by respecting varying spiritual preferences, family involvement patterns, and decision-making styles without imposing personal cultural biases.

Unit III: Ethical Principles & Theories

     

1. Ethical Theories in Healthcare

     
       
         

Deontology (Kantian Ethics)

         

Proposed by Immanuel Kant. Asserts that actions are intrinsically right or wrong based on moral duties, regardless of outcomes. Focuses on rules, rights, and absolute duties.

       
       
         

Utilitarianism (Consequentialism)

         

Proposed by Bentham & Mill. Focuses on outcomes: an action is right if it produces the "greatest good for the greatest number of people" (utility maximization).

       
       
         

Virtue Ethics

         

Traced to Aristotle. Focuses on the internal character and moral virtue of the care provider (e.g., honesty, compassion, integrity) rather than specific rules.

       
       
         

Feminist / Care Ethics

         

Focuses on relationships, empathy, contextual care, and responsiveness rather than rigid abstract rules or individualistic rights.

3. Steps of the Systematic Ethical Decision-Making Model

     
           
  1. Step 1: Assessment / Data Gathering: Collect medical facts, patient values, decision-making capacity, family perspectives, and legal boundaries.
    2.        
  2. Step 2: Identify the Ethical Dilemma: State the moral conflict clearly (e.g., Autonomy vs. Beneficence).
    3.        
  3. Step 3: Explore Courses of Action (Options): Brainstorm all viable alternatives, evaluating risks and benefits for each choice.
    4.        
  4. Step 4: Select and Implement Action: Choose the most ethically justifiable solution and execute it with interprofessional collaboration.
    5.        
  5. Step 5: Evaluate Outcomes: Assess the results, address moral distress, and gather insights for future clinical cases.
    6.      
     
        💡 SEQ Exam Key: Beauchamp & Childress describe these principles as Prima Facie duties—meaning binding obligations unless overridden by a stronger, competing moral duty in a specific scenario.

Unit IV: Confidentiality & Informed Consent

     

1. Definitions & Clinical Importance

     

Confidentiality: The ethical and legal duty to protect private client information from unauthorized disclosure.

     

Informed Consent: The voluntary, legal agreement given by a competent patient to undergo a specific medical procedure or research intervention after receiving full disclosure of facts, risks, benefits, and alternatives.

     

2. Essential Elements of Informed Consent

     
       
         

1. Disclosure

         

Providing full disclosure of diagnosis, treatment nature, risks, potential benefits, and viable alternatives.

       
       
         

2. Comprehension

         

Ensuring the patient understands the information provided (using appropriate language, free of heavy jargon).

       
       
         

3. Voluntariness

         

Assuring the consent is granted freely without coercion, force, undue influence, or manipulation.

       
       
         

4. Competence / Capacity

         

Verifying the legal and mental capacity of the client to make informed choices (adult, coherent, alert).

3. Nurse’s Roles and Legal Responsibilities in Consent

     
        📌 Crucial MCQ Point: The physician/surgeon is legally responsible for explaining the procedure and obtaining consent. The nurse acts as a witness to the signature, verifying that:        
             
  • The client signed the form voluntarily.
    •          
  • The signature is authentic.
    •          
  • The client appears mentally competent to sign.
    •        
        If a patient expresses confusion or lack of understanding, the nurse MUST hold the form and notify the surgeon immediately.      
     

4. Exceptions to Informed Consent & Confidentiality

     
           
  • Emergency Exception: Life-threatening emergency when the client is unconscious and no surrogate is available (Implied Consent applies).
    •        
  • Mandatory Disclosure / Duty to Warn: Confidentiality may be legally breached in cases of child/elder abuse, communicable disease reporting to public health authorities, or clear threats to harm oneself or others (Tarasoff rule).
