Unit I: Introduction to Nursing Ethics

1. Core Ethical Terminology (Definitions)

Ethics

The formal systematic study of moral beliefs, principles, and conduct; distinguishing right from wrong behavior in human actions.

Nursing Ethics

A specialized branch of bioethics that governs moral decisions, professional duties, and values specifically within the nurse-patient-system relationship.

Bio-Ethics

The broader study of ethical issues arising from advances in biology, medicine, and life sciences (e.g., organ transplantation, gene editing, end-of-life care).

Values

Enduring personal or professional beliefs about the worth, desirability, or importance of an idea, object, or behavior that guide decision-making.

Beliefs

Intellectual or emotional acceptance of something as true or real, with or without empirical evidence.

Morals

Personal or societal standards of right and wrong behavior deeply rooted in culture, religion, and family tradition.

Attitude

A mental position, feelings, or disposition toward a person, object, or situation that influences one's response.

Conflict

A struggle or clash resulting from incompatible or opposing needs, drives, values, or external demands.

Dilemma

A difficult situation in which a choice must be made between two options, both of which have undesirable consequences.

Ethical Dilemma

A complex situation where two or more moral principles conflict, and choosing one option forces the nurse to compromise another valid ethical duty.

2. Importance of Ethics in Nursing

  • Protects Vulnerable Patients: Safeguards patient autonomy and dignity when clients are incapacitated or at their most vulnerable.
  • Guides Clinical Decision-Making: Provides a structured standard for resolving complex healthcare situations without depending purely on emotional reactions.
  • Fosters Interprofessional Trust: Builds a culture of integrity, accountability, and respect between nurses, physicians, clients, and society.
  • Upholds Professional Boundaries: Defines legal and ethical parameters that prevent abuse of power, exploitation, and medical negligence.

3. Flexner's Criteria of a Profession Applied to Nursing

According to Flexner & Bixler, a true profession must satisfy distinct criteria:

  1. Well-Defined Body of Specialized Knowledge: Rooted in scientific theories, evidence-based nursing research, and clinical sciences.
  2. Specialized Higher Education: Academic training delivered in universities/institutions (e.g., BSN, MSN, PhD) rather than informal apprenticeships.
  3. Service Orientation & Altruism: Focuses on service to humanity and public welfare rather than financial gain alone.
  4. Autonomy and Self-Regulation: Maintains independent practice standards, licensing procedures, and professional regulatory boards (e.g., PNC).
  5. Code of Ethics: Guided by explicit moral codes (e.g., PNC & ICN Codes) that govern practitioner behaviors.
  6. Professional Organization: Represented by strong professional bodies that set policy and advance practice.

4. Characteristics of a Professional Nurse

Clinical Competence

Demonstrates mastery of clinical skills, critical thinking, and evidence-based practice to ensure client safety.

Compassion & Empathy

Delivers holistic, empathetic, and respectful care without discrimination or bias.

Accountability

Takes direct personal and legal responsibility for clinical actions, omissions, and decisions.

Integrity & Veracity

Upholds absolute honesty, maintains patient confidentiality, and remains transparent with healthcare teams.

Unit II: Value Set & Value Clarification

1. Definition of Values & Value Clarification

Value: An enduring belief about the worth or importance of a particular idea, standard, or behavior that directs decision-making.

Value Clarification: A reflective process by which individuals examine, identify, and understand their own personal values, how those values were formed, and how they influence daily behavior and professional practice.

2. Categorization / Types of Values

Value Category Definition Clinical Example
Personal Values Beliefs adopted from family, culture, and life experiences. Personal religious beliefs regarding end-of-life care.
Societal Values Values held by the broader community or society. Public right to equal, accessible healthcare access.
Organizational Values Mission, vision, and operational ethos of a health institution. Hospital policy emphasizing patient-centered excellence.
Professional Values Standards endorsed by professional nursing bodies (e.g., AACN). Altruism, Autonomy, Human Dignity, Integrity, Social Justice.
Moral Values Beliefs regarding basic principles of right vs. wrong. Honesty, non-harm, and truthfulness with clients.

3. Modes of Value Transmission

1. Modeling

Children/students learn values by observing role models (parents, senior nursing mentors).

2. Moralizing

Teaching a strict value set directly, leaving no room for the individual to question or evaluate options.

3. Laissez-Faire

Unguided exploration of values where individuals pick values with zero guidance, leading to potential value confusion.

4. Reward and Punishment

Values are reinforced through rewards for acceptable behaviors and punishment/rebuke for unacceptable ones.

5. Responsible Choice (Preferred Method)

Encourages individuals to explore multiple choices, evaluate potential consequences, and independently choose values with adult guidance.

Figure 2.1: Raths, Harmin & Simon’s 3-Step Process of Value Clarification

4. Value Conflicts and Implications to Nursing Practice

A value conflict occurs when a nurse's personal values clash with a patient's choices, institutional policies, or physician directives (e.g., personal religious objection to blood transfusions or terminal sedation).

⚠️ Clinical Implication: Unresolved value conflicts lead to moral distress, personal burnout, clinical errors, and compromised patient care. Nurses must separate personal values from professional duties to deliver non-judgmental client care.

5. Essential AACN Professional Nursing Values

  • Altruism: Unselfish concern for the welfare and well-being of clients and communities.
  • Autonomy: Respecting patient rights to self-determination and independent healthcare decisions.
  • Human Dignity: Respecting the inherent worth and uniqueness of every individual client.
  • Integrity: Acting in accordance with an appropriate code of ethics and accepted standards of practice.
  • Social Justice: Upholding moral, legal, and humanistic principles of equal treatment and fairness in healthcare access.

6. Implications of Care Ethics in a Diverse Society

In multicultural healthcare environments, nurses must practice cultural humility and transcultural care ethics by respecting varying spiritual preferences, family involvement patterns, and decision-making styles without imposing personal cultural biases.

Unit III: Ethical Principles & Theories

1. Ethical Theories in Healthcare

Deontology (Kantian Ethics)

Proposed by Immanuel Kant. Asserts that actions are intrinsically right or wrong based on moral duties, regardless of outcomes. Focuses on rules, rights, and absolute duties.

Utilitarianism (Consequentialism)

Proposed by Bentham & Mill. Focuses on outcomes: an action is right if it produces the "greatest good for the greatest number of people" (utility maximization).

Virtue Ethics

Traced to Aristotle. Focuses on the internal character and moral virtue of the care provider (e.g., honesty, compassion, integrity) rather than specific rules.

Feminist / Care Ethics

Focuses on relationships, empathy, contextual care, and responsiveness rather than rigid abstract rules or individualistic rights.

2. The Four Core Principles & Secondary Ethical Rules

Ethical Principle Clinical Definition Nursing Application Example
Autonomy Respecting a patient's right to self-determination and personal choices. Obtaining informed consent; respecting refusal of treatment.
Non-Maleficence Primum non-nocere — Above all, do no intentional or avoidable harm. Double-checking high-alert medication doses before administration.
Beneficence Actions performed for the benefit, safety, and well-being of others. Providing pain relief and turning immobile clients to prevent ulcers.
Justice Fairness in resource distribution and non-discriminatory care. Triage in emergency departments based on medical need, not wealth.
Fidelity Faithfulness, honoring promises, and maintaining professional loyalty. Returning to evaluate a patient's pain 30 minutes after medication as promised.
Veracity Absolute obligation to tell the truth without deception. Honestly informing clients about procedure risks and medical errors.
Confidentiality Respecting privacy and safeguarding protected health information. Discussing client details only in private clinical areas with authorized staff.

3. Steps of the Systematic Ethical Decision-Making Model

  1. Step 1: Assessment / Data Gathering: Collect medical facts, patient values, decision-making capacity, family perspectives, and legal boundaries.
  2. Step 2: Identify the Ethical Dilemma: State the moral conflict clearly (e.g., Autonomy vs. Beneficence).
  3. Step 3: Explore Courses of Action (Options): Brainstorm all viable alternatives, evaluating risks and benefits for each choice.
  4. Step 4: Select and Implement Action: Choose the most ethically justifiable solution and execute it with interprofessional collaboration.
  5. Step 5: Evaluate Outcomes: Assess the results, address moral distress, and gather insights for future clinical cases.
💡 SEQ Exam Key: Beauchamp & Childress describe these principles as Prima Facie duties—meaning binding obligations unless overridden by a stronger, competing moral duty in a specific scenario.

Unit IV: Confidentiality & Informed Consent

1. Definitions & Clinical Importance

Confidentiality: The ethical and legal duty to protect private client information from unauthorized disclosure.

Informed Consent: The voluntary, legal agreement given by a competent patient to undergo a specific medical procedure or research intervention after receiving full disclosure of facts, risks, benefits, and alternatives.

2. Essential Elements of Informed Consent

1. Disclosure

Providing full disclosure of diagnosis, treatment nature, risks, potential benefits, and viable alternatives.

2. Comprehension

Ensuring the patient understands the information provided (using appropriate language, free of heavy jargon).

3. Voluntariness

Assuring the consent is granted freely without coercion, force, undue influence, or manipulation.

4. Competence / Capacity

Verifying the legal and mental capacity of the client to make informed choices (adult, coherent, alert).

Figure 4.1: Distinct Roles of Physicians vs. Nurses in Informed Consent

3. Exceptions to Informed Consent

  • Emergency Exception: In life-threatening emergencies where a patient is unconscious and unable to give consent, and immediate action is required to save life or prevent permanent injury (Implied Consent).
  • Incompetent or Incapacitated Patient: When a client lacks decision-making capacity, consent must be obtained from a legally authorized surrogate decision-maker.
  • Waiver of Consent: When a competent patient voluntarily relinquishes their right to information and requests that family members make decisions for them.
  • Public Health Hazards: Mandatory treatment or isolation required by law for highly contagious, dangerous communicable diseases.

4. Legal & Ethical Boundaries of Confidentiality (HIPAA & Exceptions)

Nurses have a strict legal duty to safeguard health records. However, confidentiality is not absolute and must be breached under specific legally mandated circumstances:

  1. Suspected Child Abuse or Elder Abuse: Mandatory reporting to legal/protective authorities.
  2. Gunshot Wounds or Violent Crimes: Required reporting to law enforcement agencies.
  3. Infectious Disease Reporting: Mandatory notification of public health departments for specific communicable diseases (e.g., TB, HIV, Hepatitis).
  4. Duty to Warn / Protect (Tarasoff Ruling): If a patient expresses credible, explicit intent to harm an identifiable third party, the healthcare team has a duty to warn the intended victim and authorities.

Unit V: Patient Rights & Professional Advocacy

1. Concept of Patient Rights

Patient Rights: The fundamental moral, legal, and human entitlements that safeguard a client's dignity, autonomy, physical integrity, and right to equitable healthcare services while receiving medical care.

2. Key Elements of the Patient Bill of Rights

Right to Respect & Dignity

Receiving considerate, respectful care free from discrimination, abuse, or disregard of personal values.

Right to Privacy & Confidentiality

Absolute protection of personal health data and privacy during examinations, treatments, and discussions.

Right to Information Access

Clear, understandable explanations regarding diagnosis, treatment plans, prognoses, and medication side effects.

Right to Refuse Treatment

The legal right of a competent adult to decline any medication, test, or surgical procedure after being informed of consequences.

3. Professional Nursing Advocacy

Advocacy: The act of speaking up for, defending, and supporting a patient's rights, health, safety, and personal choices, especially when clients are vulnerable, intimidated, or unable to advocate for themselves.

  • Acting as a bridge between the client and the multidisciplinary medical team.
  • Protecting clients from unsafe medical orders, medication errors, or institutional neglect.
  • Ensuring patients fully comprehend their treatment options before signing consent forms.
Figure 5.1: The Core Pillars of Nursing Advocacy

4. Vulnerable Patient Populations

Vulnerable Group Primary Ethical Vulnerability Nursing Responsibilities
Pediatric Clients Incomplete cognitive maturity; unable to provide true legal consent. Involve parents as surrogates; use developmentally appropriate explanations; practice assent.
Elderly / Geriatric Risk of cognitive decline, physical frailty, financial exploitation, and neglect. Assess mental capacity regularly; protect dignity; screen for elder abuse.
Psychiatric / Mentally Ill Impaired reality testing, involuntary admission status, stigma. Protect human rights; prevent chemical or physical restraint overuse; ensure least restrictive care.
Terminally Ill / End-of-Life Severe pain, loss of control, vulnerability to despair or pressure. Provide aggressive palliative comfort care; support advance directives; protect autonomy.
💡 High-Yield Exam Tip: Remember that Assent is sought from minors or impaired adults who cannot give legal consent, acknowledging their participation and agreement level in care decisions.

Unit VI: Professional Codes of Ethics & Regulation

1. Purpose of a Code of Ethics

A Code of Ethics is a formal published framework that outlines professional expectations, moral obligations, core values, and commitments of a profession to society. Its primary purposes are:

  • To serve as a standard for professional behavior and self-regulation.
  • To guide nurses through complex ethical decision-making and moral dilemmas.
  • To inform the public of the ethical commitments and standards expected of registered nurses.

2. Major Regulatory & Professional Bodies

Pakistan Nursing Council (PNC)

The statutory regulatory body governing nursing education, registration, licensure, and professional conduct standards across Pakistan.

International Council of Nurses (ICN)

A global federation representing millions of nurses worldwide, responsible for establishing the universally recognized ICN Code of Ethics for Nurses.

American Nurses Association (ANA)

Publishes the comprehensive Code of Ethics for Nurses with Interpretive Statements, setting gold standards for ethical clinical practice.

Pakistan Nursing Federation (PNF)

A professional organizational body advocating for nurse welfare, rights, and working condition improvements in Pakistan.

3. Core Elements of the ICN Code of Ethics for Nurses

The ICN Code is structured around four fundamental ethical responsibilities:

  1. Nurses and People: Primary professional responsibility is to people requiring nursing care. Promotes an environment of respect for human rights, values, customs, and spiritual beliefs.
  2. Nurses and Practice: Maintains personal accountability and competence for nursing practice; exercises judgment in accepting and delegating tasks.
  3. Nurses and the Profession: Assumes major role in determining and implementing acceptable standards of clinical nursing practice, research, and education.
  4. Nurses and Co-workers: Sustains collaborative, respectful relationships with members of health and other professions.
💡 SEQ Exam Key: When answering questions on professional regulation, clearly distinguish between PNC (legal licensing & curriculum control) and ICN/ANA (ethical codes & professional guidance frameworks).

Unit VII: Patient Autonomy, Informed Refusal & End-of-Life Decisions

1. Understanding Patient Autonomy & Informed Refusal

Autonomy in Practice: The absolute right of a competent patient to direct their own medical care, including the right to accept or refuse prescribed treatments.

Informed Refusal: The legal and ethical right of a competent adult to decline medical interventions (e.g., surgery, chemotherapy, blood transfusions), even if that refusal will result in permanent disability or death.

⚠️ Clinical Rule: A competent adult patient has the legal right to refuse treatment. Forcing treatment on a competent refusing patient constitutes medical battery. Nurses must verify patient competence, explore reasons for refusal, educate on consequences, and notify the physician immediately.

2. Advanced Directives & End-of-Life Planning

Advance Directives: Legal documents that allow individuals to state their preferences for medical care before they lose the mental capacity to make decisions.

Advance Directive Type Definition & Purpose Clinical Implication
Living Will A written legal document specifying exact medical treatments desired or refused (e.g., ventilators, tube feeding) if terminally ill or incapacitated. Activated only when the patient is certified terminal or permanently unconscious.
Durable Power of Attorney for Healthcare (Healthcare Proxy) Designates a trusted surrogate decision-maker to make medical choices if the patient becomes incapacitated. Surrogate must make decisions based on substituted judgment (what the patient would have wanted).
Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) Order A formal medical order written by a physician instructing healthcare providers not to attempt CPR if breathing or heartbeat stops. Must be documented clearly in charts; does not mean "do not treat"—all other comfort and palliative care continues.

3. Euthanasia and Assisted Suicide: Ethical & Legal Distinctions

Active Euthanasia

A clinician directly and intentionally administers a lethal dose of medication to end a patient's life (e.g., lethal injection). Illegal in most countries and strongly condemned by nursing codes.

Passive Euthanasia (Letting Die)

Withholding or withdrawing life-sustaining treatment (e.g., turning off a ventilator or stopping dialysis) allowing the underlying disease to take its natural course. Ethically and legally accepted with patient/surrogate consent.

Physician-Assisted Suicide (PAS)

A clinician prescribes a lethal dose of medication, but the patient self-administers it to end their life. Highly controversial and restricted to specific legal jurisdictions.

Palliative Sedation

Administering sedatives to relieve intractable refractory symptoms at end-of-life, even if it may indirectly shorten life (justified under the Principle of Double Effect).

Unit VIII: Ethical Dilemmas in Clinical Practice & Moral Distress

1. Anatomy of Clinical Ethical Dilemmas

An ethical dilemma arises in clinical practice when two or more morally justifiable principles compete, and no matter which choice the nurse makes, a moral duty is compromised.

Common Clinical Scenarios Leading to Dilemmas:

  • Autonomy vs. Beneficence: A competent elderly patient refuses life-saving amputation due to personal beliefs, while family and physicians demand the surgery to save their life.
  • Truth-Telling (Veracity) vs. Family Wishes: Family members request that a terminal cancer diagnosis be concealed from the patient, violating the patient's right to truth and autonomy.
  • Resource Allocation (Justice): Allocating scarce intensive care unit (ICU) beds or ventilators during mass casualties or pandemic surges.
  • Whistleblowing vs. Loyalty: Witnessing a senior physician or colleague commit a medication error or engage in unsafe patient neglect and deciding whether to report it.

2. Moral Distress in Nursing

Moral Distress: Occurs when a nurse knows the ethically correct action to take, but institutional constraints, administrative pressure, legal fear, or physician opposition prevent them from executing that action.

Causes of Moral Distress

Prolonging painful end-of-life treatments due to family pressure (futile care); witnessing unsafe staffing ratios; lack of institutional support.

Consequences & Burnout

Emotional exhaustion, depression, loss of empathy, clinical errors, and physical nurse burnout leading to high turnover rates in healthcare.

3. Strategies for Resolving Ethical Dilemmas in Clinical Settings

  1. Utilize Institutional Ethics Committees (IEC): Multidisciplinary committees (composed of physicians, nurses, ethicists, legal advisors, and clergy) available to review complex cases, provide unbiased consultation, and mediate disputes.
  2. Open Interprofessional Dialogue: Conduct structured family-care conferences to align goals of care between healthcare providers, clients, and surrogates.
  3. Reflective Practice & Debriefing: Engage in regular clinical debriefings to process moral distress, review decisions objectively, and promote psychological resilience.
  4. Conscientious Objection: The right of a nurse to decline participation in a procedure (e.g., abortion or assisted suicide) that violates their deep moral or religious convictions, provided patient care is safely transferred to another qualified provider without abandonment.
💡 Master SEQ Exam Summary: When addressing clinical case studies in your SEQs, always follow a structured format: (1) Identify the core ethical principles involved, (2) State the exact dilemma, (3) Apply the 5-step ethical decision-making model, and (4) Propose an interprofessional resolution plan involving patient advocacy and ethics committee consultation.
