Unit III

Ethical Principles and Theories

In biomedical ethics, ethical theories provide the underlying foundational philosophies, while ethical principles act as practical, framework-based guidelines applied in direct clinical nursing care.

Figure 3.1: The Four Core Biomedical Principles (Beauchamp & Childress, 2019)

The Four Core Principles

1. Autonomy

Respecting the self-determination of competent patients to make their own choices regarding healthcare decisions (e.g., Informed Consent, Refusal of Treatment).

2. Non-Maleficence

"Primum non-nocere" — Above all, do no harm. Avoiding deliberate harm, risk of harm, or injury during medical interventions.

3. Beneficence

Actions done for the benefit of others. Balancing benefits against risks and costs to promote the well-being of the patient.

4. Justice

Fairness in the distribution of healthcare resources, equal treatment under similar medical circumstances, and non-discrimination.

Connecting Principles to Ethical Theories

  • Deontology (Kantian Ethics): Asserts that actions are intrinsically right or wrong, regardless of consequences. Directly aligns with Autonomy and Justice through duty-bound respect for personhood.
  • Utilitarianism (Consequentialism): Focuses on the "greatest good for the greatest number." Directly underlies Beneficence and Non-Maleficence by maximizing utility and minimizing overall harm.
  • Virtue Ethics: Emphasizes internal moral character traits (compassion, honesty, fidelity) in healthcare providers.
📌 Key Exam Point: Beauchamp & Childress define these 4 principles as Prima Facie duties—meaning they are binding unless they conflict with an equal or stronger moral obligation in a specific situation.

Ethical Dilemmas Faced by Nurses and Clients

An ethical dilemma occurs when a nurse is faced with two or more morally justifiable options, but choosing one inevitably results in violating another moral principle.

  • Autonomy vs. Paternalism: A competent client refuses a life-saving blood transfusion (e.g., Jehovah's Witness), but the care team wants to save their life.
  • Truth-Telling (Veracity) vs. Non-Maleficence: Family requests withholding a terminal cancer diagnosis from an elderly patient to avoid psychological distress.
  • Resource Allocation (Justice): Allocating limited ICU beds or ventilator access during public health surges or high patient density.
  • Moral Distress in Nurses: When nurses know the morally correct action to take but are hindered from doing so by institutional policy or physician orders.
Figure 3.2: Ethical Dilemma - Balancing Competing Moral Duties

Strategies to Resolve Ethical Dilemmas

  1. Institutional Ethics Committees (IEC): Interdisciplinary boards (nurses, physicians, ethicists, legal counsel) that review complex cases, provide consultations, and support family-team consensus.
  2. Ethics Rounds and Debriefs: Scheduled clinical meetings to discuss ongoing moral concerns, reduce moral distress, and improve interprofessional communication.
  3. Use of Professional Codes of Ethics: Referring to standard professional frameworks (ICN Code of Ethics / ANA Code) to guide boundaries and duties.
  4. Value Clarification: Assisting clients and family members to identify and articulate their personal values, religious beliefs, and preferences.
  5. Open Interprofessional Communication: Establishing shared goals of care early through family conferences and clear document reviews.

Steps of the Ethical Decision-Making Model

Beauchamp & Childress highlight a structured step-by-step framework adapted for nursing practice, closely mirroring the Nursing Process (ADPIE):

Figure 3.3: Systematic Ethical Decision-Making Flowchart

Step 1: Assessment (Gather Information)

Collect clinical facts, medical diagnosis, patient mental capacity, family wishes, cultural values, and legal parameters.

Step 2: Identify the Ethical Issue

Clearly state the conflict (e.g., Autonomy vs. Beneficence) and determine which principles are in opposition.

Step 3: Consider Courses of Action

Brainstorm all possible interventions, analyzing potential outcomes, benefits, and risks for each choice.

Step 4: Select and Implement

Choose the most ethically justifiable action plan, collaborate with the healthcare team, and carry it out with empathy.

Step 5: Evaluate the Outcome

Review the consequences of the decision, assess client/family adaptation, and identify lesson learned for future clinical cases.
