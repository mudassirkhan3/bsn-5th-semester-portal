Unit I: Introduction to Nursing Ethics

1. Core Ethical Terminology (Definitions)

Ethics

The formal systematic study of moral beliefs, principles, and conduct; distinguishing right from wrong behavior in human actions.

Nursing Ethics

A specialized branch of bioethics that governs moral decisions, professional duties, and values specifically within the nurse-patient-system relationship.

Bio-Ethics

The broader study of ethical issues arising from advances in biology, medicine, and life sciences (e.g., organ transplantation, gene editing, end-of-life care).

Values

Enduring personal or professional beliefs about the worth, desirability, or importance of an idea, object, or behavior that guide decision-making.

Beliefs

Intellectual or emotional acceptance of something as true or real, with or without empirical evidence.

Morals

Personal or societal standards of right and wrong behavior deeply rooted in culture, religion, and family tradition.

Attitude

A mental position, feelings, or disposition toward a person, object, or situation that influences one's response.

Conflict

A struggle or clash resulting from incompatible or opposing needs, drives, values, or external demands.

Dilemma

A difficult situation in which a choice must be made between two options, both of which have undesirable consequences.

Ethical Dilemma

A complex situation where two or more moral principles conflict, and choosing one option forces the nurse to compromise another valid ethical duty.

2. Importance of Ethics in Nursing

  • Protects Vulnerable Patients: Safeguards patient autonomy and dignity when clients are incapacitated or at their most vulnerable.
  • Guides Clinical Decision-Making: Provides a structured standard for resolving complex healthcare situations without depending purely on emotional reactions.
  • Fosters Interprofessional Trust: Builds a culture of integrity, accountability, and respect between nurses, physicians, clients, and society.
  • Upholds Professional Boundaries: Defines legal and ethical parameters that prevent abuse of power, exploitation, and medical negligence.

3. Flexner's Criteria of a Profession Applied to Nursing

According to Flexner & Bixler, a true profession must satisfy distinct criteria:

  1. Well-Defined Body of Specialized Knowledge: Rooted in scientific theories, evidence-based nursing research, and clinical sciences.
  2. Specialized Higher Education: Academic training delivered in universities/institutions (e.g., BSN, MSN, PhD) rather than informal apprenticeships.
  3. Service Orientation & Altruism: Focuses on service to humanity and public welfare rather than financial gain alone.
  4. Autonomy and Self-Regulation: Maintains independent practice standards, licensing procedures, and professional regulatory boards (e.g., PNC).
  5. Code of Ethics: Guided by explicit moral codes (e.g., PNC & ICN Codes) that govern practitioner behaviors.
  6. Professional Organization: Represented by strong professional bodies that set policy and advance practice.

4. Characteristics of a Professional Nurse

Clinical Competence

Demonstrates mastery of clinical skills, critical thinking, and evidence-based practice to ensure client safety.

Compassion & Empathy

Delivers holistic, empathetic, and respectful care without discrimination or bias.

Accountability

Takes direct personal and legal responsibility for clinical actions, omissions, and decisions.

Integrity & Veracity

Upholds absolute honesty, maintains patient confidentiality, and remains transparent with healthcare teams.

💡 High-Yield Exam Tip: Remember for MCQs: Ethics is formal, system-level, and theoretical; Morals are personal, individual, and culturally acquired.

Unit II: Value Set & Value Clarification

1. Definition of Values & Value Clarification

Value: An enduring belief about the worth or importance of a particular idea, standard, or behavior that directs decision-making.

Value Clarification: A reflective process by which individuals examine, identify, and understand their own personal values, how those values were formed, and how they influence daily behavior and professional practice.

2. Categorization / Types of Values

Value Category Definition Clinical Example
Personal Values Beliefs adopted from family, culture, and life experiences. Personal religious beliefs regarding end-of-life care.
Societal Values Values held by the broader community or society. Public right to equal, accessible healthcare access.
Organizational Values Mission, vision, and operational ethos of a health institution. Hospital policy emphasizing patient-centered excellence.
Professional Values Standards endorsed by professional nursing bodies (e.g., AACN). Altruism, Autonomy, Human Dignity, Integrity, Social Justice.
Moral Values Beliefs regarding basic principles of right vs. wrong. Honesty, non-harm, and truthfulness with clients.

3. Modes of Value Transmission

1. Modeling

Children/students learn values by observing role models (parents, senior nursing mentors).

2. Moralizing

Teaching a strict value set directly, leaving no room for the individual to question or evaluate options.

3. Laissez-Faire

Unguided exploration of values where individuals pick values with zero guidance, leading to potential value confusion.

4. Reward and Punishment

Values are reinforced through rewards for acceptable behaviors and punishment/rebuke for unacceptable ones.

5. Responsible Choice (Preferred Method)

Encourages individuals to explore multiple choices, evaluate potential consequences, and independently choose values with adult guidance.

Figure 2.1: Raths, Harmin & Simon’s 3-Step Process of Value Clarification

4. Value Conflicts and Implications to Nursing Practice

A value conflict occurs when a nurse's personal values clash with a patient's choices, institutional policies, or physician directives (e.g., personal religious objection to blood transfusions or terminal sedation).

⚠️ Clinical Implication: Unresolved value conflicts lead to moral distress, personal burnout, clinical errors, and compromised patient care. Nurses must separate personal values from professional duties to deliver non-judgmental client care.

5. Essential AACN Professional Nursing Values

  • Altruism: Unselfish concern for the welfare and well-being of clients and communities.
  • Autonomy: Respecting patient rights to self-determination and independent healthcare decisions.
  • Human Dignity: Respecting the inherent worth and uniqueness of every individual client.
  • Integrity: Acting in accordance with an appropriate code of ethics and accepted standards of practice.
  • Social Justice: Upholding moral, legal, and humanistic principles of equal treatment and fairness in healthcare access.

6. Implications of Care Ethics in a Diverse Society

In multicultural healthcare environments, nurses must practice cultural humility and transcultural care ethics by respecting varying spiritual preferences, family involvement patterns, and decision-making styles without imposing personal cultural biases.

Unit III: Ethical Principles & Theories

1. Ethical Theories in Healthcare

Deontology (Kantian Ethics)

Proposed by Immanuel Kant. Asserts that actions are intrinsically right or wrong based on moral duties, regardless of outcomes. Focuses on rules, rights, and absolute duties.

Utilitarianism (Consequentialism)

Proposed by Bentham & Mill. Focuses on outcomes: an action is right if it produces the "greatest good for the greatest number of people" (utility maximization).

Virtue Ethics

Traced to Aristotle. Focuses on the internal character and moral virtue of the care provider (e.g., honesty, compassion, integrity) rather than specific rules.

Feminist / Care Ethics

Focuses on relationships, empathy, contextual care, and responsiveness rather than rigid abstract rules or individualistic rights.

2. The Four Core Principles & Secondary Ethical Rules

Ethical Principle Clinical Definition Nursing Application Example
Autonomy Respecting a patient's right to self-determination and personal choices. Obtaining informed consent; respecting refusal of treatment.
Non-Maleficence Primum non-nocere — Above all, do no intentional or avoidable harm. Double-checking high-alert medication doses before administration.
Beneficence Actions performed for the benefit, safety, and well-being of others. Providing pain relief and turning immobile clients to prevent ulcers.
Justice Fairness in resource distribution and non-discriminatory care. Triage in emergency departments based on medical need, not wealth.
Fidelity Faithfulness, honoring promises, and maintaining professional loyalty. Returning to evaluate a patient's pain 30 minutes after medication as promised.
Veracity Absolute obligation to tell the truth without deception. Honestly informing clients about procedure risks and medical errors.
Confidentiality Respecting privacy and safeguarding protected health information. Discussing client details only in private clinical areas with authorized staff.

3. Steps of the Systematic Ethical Decision-Making Model

  1. Step 1: Assessment / Data Gathering: Collect medical facts, patient values, decision-making capacity, family perspectives, and legal boundaries.
  2. Step 2: Identify the Ethical Dilemma: State the moral conflict clearly (e.g., Autonomy vs. Beneficence).
  3. Step 3: Explore Courses of Action (Options): Brainstorm all viable alternatives, evaluating risks and benefits for each choice.
  4. Step 4: Select and Implement Action: Choose the most ethically justifiable solution and execute it with interprofessional collaboration.
  5. Step 5: Evaluate Outcomes: Assess the results, address moral distress, and gather insights for future clinical cases.
💡 SEQ Exam Key: Beauchamp & Childress describe these principles as Prima Facie duties—meaning binding obligations unless overridden by a stronger, competing moral duty in a specific scenario.

Unit IV: Confidentiality & Informed Consent

1. Definitions & Clinical Importance

Confidentiality: The ethical and legal duty to protect private client information from unauthorized disclosure.

Informed Consent: The voluntary, legal agreement given by a competent patient to undergo a specific medical procedure or research intervention after receiving full disclosure of facts, risks, benefits, and alternatives.

2. Essential Elements of Informed Consent

1. Disclosure

Providing full disclosure of diagnosis, treatment nature, risks, potential benefits, and viable alternatives.

2. Comprehension

Ensuring the patient understands the information provided (using appropriate language, free of heavy jargon).

3. Voluntariness

Assuring the consent is granted freely without coercion, force, undue influence, or manipulation.

4. Competence / Capacity

Verifying the legal and mental capacity of the client to make informed choices (adult, coherent, alert).

Figure 4.1: Distinct Roles of Provider vs. Nurse in Informed Consent

3. Nurse’s Roles and Legal Responsibilities in Consent

📌 Crucial MCQ Point: The physician/surgeon is legally responsible for explaining the procedure and obtaining consent. The nurse acts as a witness to the signature, verifying that:
  • The client signed the form voluntarily.
  • The signature is authentic.
  • The client appears mentally competent to sign.
If a patient expresses confusion or lack of understanding, the nurse MUST hold the form and notify the surgeon immediately.

4. Exceptions to Informed Consent & Confidentiality

  • Emergency Exception: Life-threatening emergency when the client is unconscious and no surrogate is available (Implied Consent applies).
  • Mandatory Disclosure / Duty to Warn: Confidentiality may be legally breached in cases of child/elder abuse, communicable disease reporting to public health authorities, or clear threats to harm oneself or others (Tarasoff rule).

Unit V: Bills of Rights

1. Definition of Rights & Patient's Bill of Rights

Rights: Justifiable claims, entitlements, or legal protections guaranteed to individuals by law, ethics, or societal standards.

Patient’s Bill of Rights: A formal document outlining the entitlements, guarantees, and responsibilities that clients possess while receiving care within a healthcare facility.

2. Classification of Rights

Moral Rights

Rights derived from ethical principles and human dignity (e.g., right to be treated with respect and privacy), irrespective of statutory law.

Legal Rights

Rights established, enforced, and protected by official state/national legislation (e.g., right to informed consent, right to access medical records).

3. Essential Components of Patient Rights

  • Right to respectful, non-discriminatory care regardless of race, gender, religion, or socio-economic status.
  • Right to complete, understandable information regarding diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment options.
  • Right to accept or refuse any medical intervention or treatment plan.
  • Right to privacy, confidentiality, and security of health data.
  • Right to continuity of care and clear discharge instructions.
  • Right to inspect and receive copies of medical bills and health records.

4. Consumer Rights in Healthcare & Patient Responsibilities

Patients are healthcare consumers with rights to quality, safe care. However, patients also bear key responsibilities:

  1. Providing accurate and complete medical history to the clinical team.
  2. Following agreed-upon treatment plans and clinical instructions.
  3. Fulfilling financial obligations associated with healthcare services.
  4. Showing mutual respect to healthcare staff and other patients.
💡 Exam Tip: Patient rights are not absolute; they can be limited when exercising them directly harms public health or safety (e.g., mandatory quarantine during epidemic outbreaks).

Unit VI: Professional Code of Ethics

1. Purpose of Professional Codes of Ethics

A Code of Ethics serves as an explicit, public declaration of a profession's values, standards, and moral obligations to society. It guides practice, establishes self-regulation, and provides a benchmark for evaluating professional conduct.

2. International Council of Nurses (ICN) Code of Ethics

The ICN Code outlines four fundamental responsibilities for nurses across the world:

1. To Promote Health

Fostering healthy lifestyles and wellness across individuals and communities.

2. To Prevent Illness

Educating clients, immunizations, infection control, and health screening initiatives.

3. To Restore Health

Delivering acute and chronic therapeutic nursing interventions to ill clients.

4. To Alleviate Suffering

Providing compassionate palliative, end-of-life, and comfort-focused care.

The Four Elements of the ICN Code:

  • Nurses and People: Primary duty is to individuals needing nursing care; respecting rights, values, and privacy.
  • Nurses and Practice: Maintaining personal competence, lifelong learning, and professional standards.
  • Nurses and the Profession: Participating in setting practice standards, clinical research, and policy development.
  • Nurses and Co-workers: Sustaining collaborative, respectful relationships with interprofessional team members.

3. Pakistan Nursing Council (PNC) Code of Ethics

The PNC Code regulates nursing practice within Pakistan, enforcing professional compliance, regulatory licensing standards, and ethical boundaries across all healthcare settings.

PNC Ethical Standard Clinical Focus
Professional Accountability Nurses are individually responsible for their clinical judgments and actions.
Cultural & Religious Sensitivity Delivering non-judgmental care aligned with local socio-cultural and Islamic values.
Safeguarding Confidentiality Strict protection of client health data except under explicit legal requirement.
Maintenance of Competence Mandatory engagement in Continuing Professional Development (CPD) for license renewal.

Unit VII: Autonomy as an Ethical Issue

1. Definition of Autonomy & Paternalism

Autonomy: The freedom and capability of an individual to make independent choices regarding their own life and healthcare decisions.

Paternalism: Overriding a competent patient's choices or autonomy for their own perceived benefit, acting like a protective parent figure.

2. Spectrum of Autonomy vs. Paternalism

Figure 7.1: Shift from Historical Paternalism to Modern Patient Autonomy

3. Factors Influencing Autonomy

Internal Factors

Age, cognitive maturity, decision-making capacity, psychological state, and level of consciousness.

External Factors

Family pressure, socio-cultural expectations, financial constraints, institutional policies, and health literacy.

4. Professional Practice Dilemmas Related to Autonomy

  • Refusal of Life-Saving Treatment: When a competent patient refuses blood transfusions or chemotherapy.
  • Non-Compliance / Non-Adherence: Patients choosing lifestyle options that directly contradict medical advice.
  • Surrogate Decision-Making: Family members making choices that contradict the expressed wishes of an incapacitated patient.
⚠️ Clinical Guidance: Modern bioethics prioritizes Patient Autonomy over Paternalism. A mentally competent adult patient has the legal and ethical right to refuse any medical treatment, even if that refusal leads to death.

Unit VIII: Ethical Practice Dilemmas & Special Topics

1. End-of-Life Ethical Dilemmas

DNR (Do Not Resuscitate)

A medical order instructing healthcare providers not to attempt CPR if the patient experiences cardiac or respiratory arrest. Requires clear documentation and/or valid patient/surrogate consent.

Euthanasia

Active: Direct action to end life (e.g., lethal injection). Passive: Withholding life-sustaining measures. Illegal in Pakistan; legally and ethically restricted worldwide.

Advance Directives

Legal documents detailing a person's healthcare preferences (Living Will, Durable Power of Attorney for Healthcare) executed before losing decision-making capacity.

Palliative Care vs. Terminal Sedation

Focusing on comfort, symptom management, and dignity without actively hastening or intentionally causing death (Principle of Double Effect).

2. Organ Transplantation & Allocation Ethics

Organ allocation involves severe scarcity. Ethical dilemmas surround distribution criteria:

  • Medical Need & Urgency: Prioritizing patients closest to organ failure death.
  • Benefit & Success Probability: Giving preference to candidates with higher post-transplant survival prospects.
  • Justice vs. Market Exploitation: Preventing organ commercialism/trafficking and ensuring equitable access regardless of financial status.

3. Reproductive & Genetic Ethics

Topic Ethical Considerations
Abortion Conflict between fetal right to life vs. maternal autonomy/health. Evaluated through legal, religious, and ethical frameworks.
Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART / IVF) Status of unused frozen embryos, surrogate maternity rights, and genetic screening.
Genetic Testing & Editing Concerns over genetic discrimination, privacy of genomic data, and ethical boundaries of CRISPR gene modification.

4. Moral Distress & Whistleblowing in Nursing

Moral Distress: Occurs when a nurse knows the ethically correct action to take, but is blocked from acting by institutional constraints, physician orders, or legal barriers.

Whistleblowing: Reporting illegal, unethical, or unsafe practices within an organization to external authorities or leadership. Requires courage, documentation, and protection under whistle-blower standards.
